After reading the three different articles, I see a significant connection among them, specifically how each one highlights a key detail relevant to the themes of this class. The first article talks about Indymedia.org which is still today a website where people combined their voices on a shared platform with the aim of making an impact as a community and bringing about positive change. In contrast, today people raise their voices through various platforms, such as Instagram, TikTok, Facebook, and X, but often with the expectation motive of receiving something in return, whether that be money, fame, or views. Indymedia.org, on the other hand, existed for the public good to unite the community and its people, particularly when the objective was to put an end to a specific problem. Like the ICE protesting theme happening now. Not only that, but people were free to express themselves and write whatever was on their minds completely free of charge and with pure intentions without resorting to hate speech, racism, or content with a negative impact; their voices were truly heard and given noticeable form. Nowadays, it is true that there are many platforms and websites where a news item or a written account of an event can potentially go viral. Today I feel people are constantly in competition; whoever posts something first that goes viral expects to receive money, fame, or views in exchange. Not really caring for others, and what can be affected. This brings to mind Project #2 the assignment we just completed in this class. It was not merely a simple project, but rather an example of "emergence" in art: a multitude of small contributions giving rise to a final work that no single individual could have fully predicted in advance. Yet, thanks to communication and collective effort, the project evolved into a functional entity. If developed further in the future, a project like this could be taken to the next level, integrating it into the digital space and transformed into a public network designed to further unite the community. It could become a manageable and accessible project free from discriminatory biases or design barriers, empowering every individual contributor to help create new, unexpected, and collective experiences.

In the past, the Internet was more human, smaller in scale, and more grounded in solidarity. The second article suggests that we might need to learn from older communities such as Echo, if we wish for online spaces to once again feel safer, kinder, and more meaningful. Echo did not aspire to be wealthy or massive; its goal was simply to be a good community. The article postulate that modern social networks have lost that spirit. Consequently, it leads me to believe that the future of the Internet is, quite obviously, a matter of choice. The article argues that the Internet did not have to evolve into what it is today. People made decisions that led to the creation of gigantic platforms. In the future, we can make different decisions. For example: A city can choose to build either massive highways or smaller, or instead walkable neighborhoods. Something similar applies to the Internet: everything depends on what people decide to build. Since the future depends on us, a new generation in which artificial intelligence is present everywhere today but is up to us whether we place 100% of our trust in AI, or decide instead to place 100% of our trust in ourselves and our own artistic skills to build sites, designs, and platforms that are entirely secure and fundamentally human-centric.

Another idea that got me hooked up is that social networks should be smaller. Perhaps, the best future does not lie in a single giant platform, but rather in many small communities based on region, shared interests, or common values. For example: Instead of a single app for the entire world, imagine many smaller spaces, one for local artists, another for science fiction enthusiasts, another for residents of Brooklyn, and so on. Because building a giant platform or a massive website, it becomes increasingly difficult to tend to the internal networks and all the elements that comprise them, once they reach an excessive scale; a platform with billions of users simply cannot be perceived as being as personal, secure, or accountable as one of a smaller scope. For instance: it is easy to manage a classroom of 20 students; it is far more difficult to look after a packed stadium of 20,000 people with the same level of personal attention. 

Therefore, I believe that as website creators, we must carefully consider the intentions behind the sites we build; for the larger a platform becomes, the more difficult and complex it becomes to manage the entire system, both internally and externally. Internally, this concerns the coding and resources required to ensure everything functions properly; externally, it involves ensuring the platform meets user expectations and provides secure, clear, manageable and reliable access while browsing the site.
