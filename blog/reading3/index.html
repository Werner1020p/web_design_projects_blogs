Reading 3

  • 18 March 2026

What struck me most about these readings is that they all challenge the fantasy that digital technology is fluid, neutral, and in some way detached from real life. Frank Chimero made me reflect on the web as a material with its own inherent logic, rather than simply a blank canvas upon which designers can impose whatever ideas they wish. Chimero says the web is fluid and vertical, and that it reaches its full potential only when we work in harmony with its elemental nature, this made me realize that good design is not about controlling everything, but rather about understanding the medium in which one works, and the sensation it conveys to both the reader and the creator. Ted Chiang’s says something similar, an idea from a different perspective: ChatGPT may sound intelligent and polished, but that does not mean it truly comprehends what it is saying. His "blurry JPEG" metaphor stuck with me, as it explains why AI can appear convincing even when it simplifies, remixes, or fabricates information. Honestly, this was striking because it led me to question the tool itself, how we use/face AI these days, and the extent to which it technically influences everything around us. Like almost everywhere you go and everywhere you navigate into the digital world we might end up encountering IA. It made me consider how sensitive our brains are to color, information, and interactivity, so much so that we are led to believe the information provided by ChatGPT or any AI is perfectly balanced and generated with the outmost sophistication. Yet, this tells me to cast doubt on the interactive experience AI delivers, information that may be 100% guaranteed in terms of delivery, but is not always 100% real or factually accurate. This had me to reflect more critically on the distinction between something that merely looks good online and something that is, in reality, reliable or well-constructed.

What I found most interesting is that the third article extends this concept which reaches both design and AI to the realm of memory itself. Heavy Lies the Digital Cloud led me to conceive of the "cloud" in a way I had never considered before. Generally, the concept of "the cloud" sounds harmless, almost comforting; it resembles a neutral storage space for our photos, notes, playlists, and messages. However, in reality, it is a system in which our memories are stored, categorized, and interpreted by companies whose priorities are profit, prediction, and control. That idea unsettled me. It made me realize that digital memory is not solely about safeguarding the past; it also entails determining who holds the authority to organize that past, which elements are preserved, and which aspects are transformed into data for future decision making. Even something we perceive as personal, such as a playlist or an old photo roll, can be integrated into a larger scale system that, in turn, shapes the way we perceive ourselves. I pay Apple iCloud every month specifically to gain more storage space, to save more photos, more videos, and more of the moments I’ve lived. But after reading these articles, I now feel uneasy; personally, it leads me to suspect that I am not merely paying a monthly fee for what might seem like a harmless tool, but am instead feeding information into a system that could be monitoring me through my activities, photo galleries, and memories things I consider private to myself, but which, to the system, may not be. I honestly find myself questioning whether I should continue paying for Apple iCloud every month whether it is truly worth it, and whether it poses or transmits any future risk to my privacy.

The three readings: they all demonstrate that digital systems are never "mere tools." The web possesses its own texture; AI, its own distortions; and the cloud, its own power. This notion proves particularly relevant in the context of a web design class, as it reminds me that design is not limited to creating visually appealing interfaces; rather, it also entails understanding the structures underlying those interfaces and questioning the effects they apply on people. Prior to these readings, I perceived the internet in a more superficial manner primarily through applications, websites, and visual elements. Now, I reflect more deeply on the hidden rules that underpin those experiences: what the medium enables, what gets compressed, and who benefits from the way the provided information is stored and presented. The readings got me with the questions: If digital systems are constantly shaping meaning, memory, and attention, what responsibility, then, do designers bear when building within those systems? And what are their true intentions especially these days where everything might see artificial?
